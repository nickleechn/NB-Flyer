# MB Flyer Script Writing

Write as the presenter talking to a friend about a trip they actually took. Ground the story in the footage and the creator's account. The user's current instructions override the defaults below.

## Establish the trip from evidence

Read existing drafts and corrections before starting over. Identify the supplied clip range and inspect it before drafting: use labelled contact sheets for coverage, sample several points in long takes, and inspect the relevant video or audio for events that still images cannot establish. Do not claim to have watched material you only sampled.

Build a compact private working map of the journey: locations, transport legs, service numbers, times, seating or cabin classes, and the clips supporting each beat. Distinguish what is visible, what the creator recalls, and what needs verification. Keep research and edit notes outside the spoken script.

Resolve dictation errors in proper nouns using footage and context. A destination printed on a departure board may be the service terminus rather than the passenger's stop. If later notes conflict with the initial itinerary, reconcile the evidence; ask one focused question only when the conflict materially changes the story and remains unresolved.

## Write for the ear

Follow the journey naturally: getting there, booking or boarding, the experience on board, time at the destination, and the return when relevant. Use only the beats the trip supports. Open with the actual plan, without a grand introduction.

Use contractions, varied sentence lengths, occasional fragments, and simple transitions such as “Once we got inside…” or “On the way back…”. Let practical details carry the story. Do not manufacture slang, filler, reactions, or personal experiences to make the script sound human.

Prefer specific observations to generic praise. “We had just sat down when the train pulled out” is more useful than “Japan's world-class rail system delivered exceptional punctuality.” One on-time departure does not establish that all trains are always punctual.

Avoid brochure language, repeated summaries, formulaic comparisons, and a takeaway section by default. End with the final moment of the trip and a brief natural sign-off if appropriate. Add a verdict or call to action only when requested or already part of the creator's brief.

Write enough to match the available visual coverage. Do not invent scenes to fill a target runtime. If narration is already approved or recorded, preserve its wording and timing unless the requested revision changes it; flag any new mismatch for the edit.

## Check facts without turning narration into a lecture

Verify checkable claims using footage and current primary sources: operators, stations, hotels, official timetables, booking providers, and official statistics. Keep citations in a separate fact-check note.

Pay particular attention to distinctions that commonly get blurred:

- Service name versus vehicle model or subtype; identify the subtype from a clear marking or reliable evidence rather than appearance alone.
- Maximum permitted speed versus the speed actually observed on this leg.
- Population rank versus metropolitan-area rank, and station building size versus passenger volume.
- Reserved and non-reserved ticket rules, including date-specific exceptions.
- A personal ticketing problem versus a general rule about a booking platform.

Keep useful context brief in narration. Qualify uncertain claims or omit them; never silently turn a guess into a fact. Preserve the creator's experience without extrapolating it into a universal guarantee.

## Revise in place and deliver Markdown

Maintain one clearly named current script `.md` file so the creator can review it beside the chat. Incorporate incremental notes into the relevant passage instead of appending a list of disconnected additions. Keep the route, names, tense, and chronology consistent across the whole draft.

The script should be ready to read aloud. Put sources, unresolved questions, and optional shot suggestions in a separate notes file when needed. Estimate runtime from the word count and intended delivery rate, labelling it as an estimate until audio exists.

Before delivering, read it through for spoken rhythm, repetition, unsupported facts, awkward transitions, and coverage gaps. Confirm that the ending follows the brief rather than adding an unsolicited lesson.

## Optional review and voiceover handoff

When the user requests an external model review, send the current narration with a focused brief: preserve facts, chronology, viewpoint and meaning; make the rhythm conversational; add no experiences or claims. Use the requested provider and model only if available, and report any substitution. Review the returned text before adopting it. Do not say a service reviewed the script unless the request succeeded.

For authorised voice generation, export a separate narration-only `.txt`: omit headings, citations, stage directions and review notes. Expand ambiguous numbers, times and model names for pronunciation where needed without changing the displayed script's meaning. Use the user's chosen voice and speed. Keep credentials outside all script, skill and repository files.

Pass the current script, narration text, generated audio if requested, and footage notes to the video edit. Do not start voice generation, music generation, publishing or editing solely because this script-writing skill is being used.
